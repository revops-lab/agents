# Cold email sequence

A sequence fails when it tries to speak to everyone. One segment, one problem, one angle, three
steps: the first campaign in most practitioner playbooks looks exactly like this, because it is
the only version you can learn from.

## Context first
Read `company-context.md`: ideal customer, buying group, buying situations, proof and voice.

## Questions (at most three)
1. "Which segment is this for? (industry, size, role, and the trigger, if any)"
2. "What is the one problem you solve for them, in their words?"
3. "What proof can we use? (a result, a customer type, a number you can stand behind)"

## Method
- **Teach, do not pitch.** Each email carries one useful idea the reader has not considered
  (the Challenger research on commercial insight). The product appears last, briefly.
- **Short.** 50 to 100 words per email. Gong's analysis shows replies drop beyond about 100
  words, and executives reply less than other roles.
- **Their situation first.** The first line is about them and why now (a trigger, a pattern in
  their industry), never about you.
- **One easy question.** Interest, not a meeting: "Worth a look?" beats "15 minutes Tuesday?".
- **Different angle per step.** Email 1: the problem and why now. Email 2: a short example or
  number from a similar company. Email 3: a smaller first step or a useful resource, and a
  polite close.
- **Timing.** Steps 3 to 4 working days apart. Stop the sequence on any reply.

## Output
1. The sequence: subject (two to four words) and body for each step, with word counts.
2. Two alternative first lines for step 1 to test.
3. What to personalise per contact (one field each, for example the trigger) and what stays fixed.
4. A send checklist: sending domain ready (see sending-domain-audit), list verified, opt-out
   line, plain text, at most one link, no attachments.
5. What to avoid for this segment.

## Guardrails
- Nothing is sent or loaded into a sequencer without approval.
- No fake familiarity, no "re:" or "quick question" subjects, no invented proof.
- No em dashes.

Research behind this skill: Gong cold email research (length, executive reply rates);
Dixon and Adamson, *The Challenger Sale* (commercial insight); Google and Yahoo sender rules.
See RESEARCH.md.
